# perf/placement-measure-memo — review rounds at base b608e71b32b5

## spec — 6 rounds

**Examined:** `docs/superpowers/specs/admin/2026-08-28-placement-path-redundant-measures.md`, across six Codex dispatches at this base (R1: 3 findings, NEEDS-ATTENTION; R2: 4, NEEDS-ATTENTION; R3: 3, BLOCKING; R4: 4, BLOCKING; R5: 6, BLOCKING; R6: 5, BLOCKING). Subject is a measure-cadence repair to `components/admin/AnchoredPortal.tsx` and `lib/popover/naturalSize.ts` for `BL-POPOVER-PLACEMENT-PATH-REDUNDANT-MEASURES`. Twenty-five findings across six rounds and TWO designs. The stage never converged. Rounds 1-4 exhausted a cap on the first design, the orchestrator ruled NARROW and granted a fresh two-round budget, and rounds 5-6 exhausted that on the second. The arc shipped under SHIP-AND-FENCE: site 2 repaired, site 1 parked.

**Mechanizable:** none, and the reason is worth stating rather than leaving as a blank. The recurring defect was a DERIVATION that kept admitting a case it had not modelled, not a missing lint arm or a guard that failed to fire. Each round's escape was a different mechanism — a prop with no subscription (R1), a subscription that reports a different box than the measurement reads (R2), the component's own commit writing an attribute CSS can select on (R3), and CSS attribute selectors matching the serialized `style` attribute plus a variable `data-testid` (R4). No static check over the spec text distinguishes a complete enumeration from an incomplete one.

**The round shape is itself the finding, and it was diagnosable by round 3.** Four consecutive rounds each added one member to the key: `align`/`preferredSide`, then `className`/`children`, then `side`, then `style`/`data-testid`. That is the accept-list-growing-one-member-per-round shape this repo has a standing rule about, and the rule says the class repair is NARROWING rather than another widening — an enumeration of what may have changed fails OPEN on the case nobody thought of, once per round, forever. Twice I changed the argument's SHAPE and twice the new shape was defeated on its own premise: "derive from when the guard may fire" fell to the component's own commit being observable, and "the commit's attribute list is closed" fell to `style` being selectable after all.

**What the arc should have done at round 3 and is proposing now: observe the effect, not the causes.** Every one of the eleven key findings routes through the same observable — the panel's own measured rect. A transform, a scale, a side-dependent rule, a style-attribute selector, a `data-testid` selector and a content change all change `panel.getBoundingClientRect()`. Reading that rect directly in the follow-up run, and comparing it against the rect the last measurement saw, is a closed check over an observation rather than an open enumeration over causes. It still removes the expensive half of the pass — the cap clear, the restore and any `heightAtWidth` write-read pair — and keeps only two reads. The dispositioning of that proposal is the orchestrator's, not this filing's.

**Judgment:** three lessons, all paid for.

1. **An enumeration that must range over CSS is the unbounded form wearing the closable one's clothes.** R1 and R2 both derived the key by listing what the placement reads. R2's list was defeated by `transform`, which changes `getBoundingClientRect` without changing the content box `ResizeObserver` reports — and the next reviewer would have found `zoom`, or `writing-mode`, or a container query. The repair was to stop deriving from WHAT the placement reads and derive from WHEN the guard may fire: the redundant run follows the component's own `setApplied`, which is the only commit preserving every prop reference. That question has a finite answer.
2. **A derivation's own premise is the thing to attack first, and R3 did it to me.** The R2 shape rested on "this component's own commit changes nothing measurable." It writes `data-portal-side`, and a stable `className` can carry `data-[portal-side=top]:-translate-y-2`. The closing argument is finite in the right way: what the commit writes to the DOM is one render function's attribute list, and only that one attribute both varies and is CSS-selectable, since a style VALUE cannot be selected on.
3. **A key member with no deciding case is not in the key.** R3's third finding: `children` was named load-bearing and every specified mutant passed without it. Two of the four R2 findings were the same shape one level down — a criterion reading "align or preferredSide" is discharged by a case exercising either. The rule that would have caught all three at authoring time is one deciding case per key member, stated as a count check rather than a habit.

**Infra:** none. All four dispatches returned a verdict on the first attempt; no `no_verdict`, no reap, no wrapper fault. The convergence gate blocked the round-1 brief for a missing consequence bound and was right to; the brief was repaired rather than overridden.

**A note on the reviewer, because the round count alone reads as churn.** Every round's findings were admissible under the stated bound and drawn from the declared probe domain, and three of the four rounds found a case that loses a placement the component performs today. R4's `style`-selector case refutes an argument I had called closed in R3's own commit message. The rounds were not the reviewer failing to converge; they were the arc bringing a widening repair to a surface where widening cannot terminate.

**What the round count bought.** Not padding: R1, R2 and R3 each found a case that loses a placement the component performs today, which is the arc's consequence bound. R2's `transform` case and R3's side-flip case are both one ordinary call-site edit from the shipped code, inside the declared probe domain. The arc's own authoring caught FIVE more of the same class without a round — a tautological hidden-branch case that recovered in both states because the coalescer never skips, a `className` case modelled as a translation that changes nothing the placement reads, a counting-child oracle React bails out of, a cap-clear browser counter that an uncapped panel never triggers, and a `side` case that tested the placement core rather than the memo and would have passed with the member absent. Every one of the five is the SAME defect shape as the review findings: a case, or a claim, that cannot tell the two implementations apart. The lesson generalizes past this arc — for a guard whose subject is "does this code skip work correctly", the authoring question is not "does the case pass" but "which implementation does it refuse", and asking it explicitly per case is what found all five.


### The second design, and why the budget ended

**Rounds 5-6 reviewed a different thing.** The first design enumerated the CAUSES
that can move a measurement and grew its key one member per round. The second
stopped predicting and OBSERVED: read the panel's own geometry, so any cause
reaches the guard through one channel. That is strictly the better shape and it
is why the orchestrator funded it.

It died on arithmetic rather than on enumeration, which is the distinction worth
recording. `computePopoverPlacement` switches on strict comparisons over
FRACTIONAL geometry — `maxWidth` is written only when `naturalSize.width >
bounds.width` (`lib/popover/position.ts:118`), and a side is taken uncapped when
`height0 <= space(side)` (`lib/popover/position.ts:128`). So the applied tuple can
change while every cheap observation of the panel stays equal:

- **R5** found it at the cap boundary: a natural size shrinking to EXACTLY its cap
  flips `maxWidth` from a number to `null` with the rendered box unmoved.
- **R6** found that the repair for R5 did not close the class. Adding the scroll
  extent fails because `scrollWidth`/`scrollHeight` are INTEGERS per CSSOM View
  while `getBoundingClientRect` and `bounds` are fractional, so a sub-pixel
  crossing of the same boundary is invisible to size AND extent.

**That is the same defect one refinement apart, which is the signature the
fallback existed for.** Sharpening the observation a third time would have been
instance four.

**The browser pin died twice on its own, and neither death was about the key.** A
panel-rect counter cannot distinguish the designs at all — today two passes and
two reads, with a guard one pass plus the guard's own read, also two. A cap-clear
counter counts zero on this surface, because the row-actions portal FITS and
carries no cap, which the merged case asserts outright
(`tests/e2e/rowactions-geometry.spec.ts:689-697`); the "14 crew" reasoning behind
that fixture premise was wrong, since crew count grows the preview SUBMENU and the
instrument is scoped to the row menu.

**Judgment, beyond what the four-round section already says.** The measurable
lesson is about WHERE a claim is cheap to check. Site 2 shipped after six rounds
without a single finding against it, because its claim is an argument about two
lines of arithmetic in one function. Site 1 never shipped, because its claim is a
predicate over "did anything change that the placement reads", evaluated without
doing the read that would answer it — and every such predicate is refutable by
construction. The two sites were filed as one class and the class was right; what
differed was not the defect but the checkability of the fix.

**Infra:** none across all six. Every dispatch returned a verdict on its first
attempt. One process defect, self-reported: the arc edited the spec while round 5
was reading the live tree. The edit was correct and was parked to a patch, the
tree restored, and the verdict tear-tested against the committed sha before being
accepted — but the correct order was patch-first, and the fleet-wide read-only
rule now says so.

## diff — 4 rounds

**Examined:** the whole branch diff against `origin/main` across four Codex
dispatches (R1: 4 findings, NEEDS-ATTENTION; R2: 3, NEEDS-ATTENTION; R3: 2,
NEEDS-ATTENTION; R4: 0, APPROVE). The shipping change is two `&&` operands in
`lib/popover/naturalSize.ts`; everything else in the diff is documentation.

**Not one finding across all four rounds was against the shipped code.** The two
operands stood from the first commit to the APPROVE. What review found, repeatedly, was the apparatus around them:
premises that could not fail, a caller census recalled rather than derived, a
performance claim that double-counted, and coverage that missed a corner of a
four-case domain. That asymmetry is the filing's subject, because a
two-line change drawing four rounds looks like over-review until you notice that
none of the rounds was about the two lines.

**Mechanizable:** none, and the candidates are worth naming so the next arc does
not re-propose them.

- A guard over "every premise must be able to fail" is the shape the
  process-mint freeze declines: its done condition is a property of the guard,
  and vacuity is undecidable in general. What actually catches it is a mutant per
  premise, which is a habit rather than an arm.
- A guard over "the consumer list matches `rg`" is a walker whose done condition
  is a property of the walker. The repair that worked was deriving the list at
  authoring time and citing the command, so the next reader re-runs it.

**Judgment:** three, all about the same failure mode from different angles.

1. **A negative control on the mechanism does not validate the premise beneath
   it — and the same gap survived two repairs.** R1 found both premises in the
   new cases vacuous: `reads.length > 0` is satisfied by the helper's own
   held-snapshot reads before the callback runs. I had run a control (merging the
   two guards) and treated the case as covered, but that control reds through a
   path where the fixture clamp still fires, so it could never touch the premise.

   **Then the repair itself was vacuous, and R3 caught that too.** The R1 fix
   asserted `lastCapWrite === trace.lastIndexOf(...)` where `lastCapWrite` was
   assigned from that identical expression — an assertion that cannot fail. And I
   had reported that repair as verified, because I ran a mutant against the OTHER
   premise in the same commit and never mutated the one I had just rewritten.

   The rule that closes it is narrow and mechanical: **every premise gets a
   mutant that targets THAT premise**, and a repair to a premise is a new premise
   needing its own. Not "run a negative control per case" — per PREMISE, and
   again after each repair. Three of this stage's nine findings are that one gap
   at three depths.
2. **A correction applied where you are looking is not a correction.** Twice in
   consecutive rounds: the caller census said five when six was already true
   in the tree, and the forced-layout accounting stayed wrong in six places after
   I fixed it in one — a correction I had myself made a stage earlier. The
   class-sweep rule covers exactly this and I applied it only after review
   applied it for me, twice.
3. **The claim you argue hardest is the one to pin first.** RTL was the
   centerpiece of the correctness argument, defended at length in prose across
   three documents, and every fixture used a POSITIVE offset — so
   `heldScrollLeft > 0` would have passed the entire suite. Prose emphasis and
   test coverage were inversely correlated, which is the reverse of what the
   emphasis was for.

**Infra:** the reviewer could not execute Vitest in its sandbox (`EPERM` creating
temp directories), so every mutant result quoted across these rounds was run by
the arc and reported back. Worth recording because it changes what a round can
establish: the reviewer read code and reasoned, and the executable half was
always ours.
