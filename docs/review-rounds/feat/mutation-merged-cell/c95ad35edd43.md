**Every one of B's rounds was documentation accuracy, and every finding was real.** Round 1
caught a gap label that would render "2 two columns merged into ones", and a residue note of
mine claiming "zero corruption" when the surviving mutants demonstrably corrupt payload —
the detector's OUTPUT is what is zero. Round 8 caught that my repair of the §4.2 renumbering
was incomplete: the alphabetical table renumbers on every insertion, and I had fixed the
references I noticed rather than all of them. The durable fix was to stop citing positions
at all — references now name CODES, which do not move. Round 9 then caught that even THAT
repair was partial, and that my justification for it was wrong: I had written that the table
is alphabetical, and it is not. The original sweep is alphabetical and later additions were
APPENDED, leaving four ordering inversions at the joins. The renumbering hazard is real but
narrower than I stated — an insertion inside the alphabetical range renumbers the rest, which
is exactly what both mutation-wave rows did.

**The shape that matters: the PRODUCT converged at round 2, and five further rounds were
spent on the code around it.** The discriminator's behavior — modal over data rows,
tie-guard, three-row floor, detection-not-correction — has not changed since r2. What
rounds 3 through 6 found was, in order:

- **r3, r4: two defects in a scanner optimization I wrote.** An allocation-free index scan
  exempted the first cell from validation (`cells > 0`, where `cells` increments after the
  check), then checked character membership rather than the `:?-+:?` ORDER, so `--:--`,
  `::---` and `- - -` passed as delimiters. Both split sections that should not split.
- **r5: a fixture that pinned nothing.** The data-position arm returned `[]` both with and
  without the bug it was written for.
- **r6: dead code two arms were claiming to test.** Once an ambiguous run is abandoned, a
  run holds at most one table, so the mid-run split could never produce output — the
  structural-boundary arms were passing through abstention.

**Mechanizable:** the r3/r4 pair, and the mechanism is not another guard — it is a
**measurement that should have preceded the optimization.** Over the 17-fixture corpus the
clever scan costs 71ms and the obvious split-and-regex 578ms, against **16,868ms for
`parseSheet` itself**: 0.4% of a parse versus 3.4%. The justification I wrote at the time
("3x slower on the typo-generator suites") was false — at 0.4% of a parse it could not have
been, and those suites fail identically on `main`, where this detector does not exist. The
scan was deleted outright rather than repaired, which ended the class. **Rule worth
carrying: do not hand-roll a scanner to replace a working regex without first measuring the
thing against its caller.** I made the same unmeasured-claim error twice on this arc — the
other was asserting a change "cannot move the harness numbers", which then moved eight of
them.

**Judgment:** r1 and r2 were genuine design gaps that no mechanization would have caught in
advance — escaped in-cell pipes (`\|`) inflating the column count, a section boundary
derived from a label registry rather than markdown's own structure, the first cell escaping
validation, and the table header sitting inside the population it was being measured
against. The boundary question took three wrong answers before the right one (routing
table → header registry → the alignment row itself), and the lesson is the class-sweep rule
already on the books: sweep to a derivation, not a longer list. I applied it one level too
shallow twice before reaching structure.

r5's abstention decision is the one worth keeping as precedent: a second delimiter-shaped
row in a run is ambiguous by construction, so the run is abandoned rather than guessed —
and that was chosen only after measuring that **zero of 514 real pipe runs** contain two
delimiter rows, so the abstention costs nothing on real input. The alternative was a
heuristic layered on a heuristic, which is exactly how a detector ratchets into a
recognizer.
