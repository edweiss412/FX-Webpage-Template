## spec — 11 rounds

**Examined:** whether four spec rounds on a ~15-line production change bought their cost, and what would have shortened the train.

**Judgment:** rounds 1 and 2 were worth their cost and then some. R1 finding 1 caught an application-level deadlock that would have hung the cron on every first-seen apply — the design passed a `shows` id that was still uncommitted inside the transaction awaiting the sink, so the FK check on the sink's separate connection would wait on a transaction waiting on it. That defect was invisible to inspection and the spec's own rationale had the causality backwards, citing the breaking case as the justification. R2 finding 5 caught a data-destruction hazard: `prune_sync_log` deletes every qualifying row in whatever database it targets, and the sink's resolver prefers `TEST_DATABASE_URL`, which on this machine is the shared validation project. Neither is the "guard does not pin what it claims" shape the round-economy retrospective identifies as the dominant late-round waste; both are real defects in shipping behavior.

**Mechanizable:** one class, and it is the reason there was an R3 at all. Three findings across two rounds were instances of a single vector — *a `sync_log` row that could name its show does not* (R1 F2 at the sink, R1 F7 in the accept-set, R2 F1 at seven callers). Each was repaired per-instance, which is precisely what `docs/agents/writing-plans.md` forbids once a class is nameable: *"the 3-round same-vector threshold is a ceiling, not a waiting period."* The class was nameable after R1 F2. The structural defense — a filesystem-walked meta-test requiring every `sync_log` call site to either pass a `drive_file_id` or hold a registry row — was designed only after R2, unprompted, and lands in the arc as `tests/sync/_metaSyncLogAttribution.test.ts`. Shipping it in the R1 repair would have made R2 F1 a red test instead of a review round.

Second mechanizable item: the bare-citation class. `pnpm spec:lint` flagged 51 malformed citations on the first run and 24 more after the R1 repair, both times because same-file shorthand (`` `:2425` ``) was reintroduced during editing. Both were swept mechanically in one pass. Running `spec:lint` before *each* repair commit, not only before dispatch, removes it entirely.

**Infra:** none. All three dispatches returned clean verdicts; no reaper kill, no `no_verdict`, no rollout scrape. The convergence gate blocked the R1 brief for stating its consequence bound in non-canonical wording — correctly, and the brief was rewritten rather than overridden, which improved it.

**Diagnosis of the residual:** the spec absorbed plan-stage material. Three of R2's six findings were about test assertions (per-sink oracle coverage, prune isolation, `cron.job.active`) — all real, all properly plan-stage. §5 had grown from an acceptance posture into an assertion-by-assertion table, and a spec that specifies assertions invites assertion-shaped findings at the stage where closing them costs a full round. The corrective is to move that content to the plan, which already carries it at higher fidelity, and leave the spec holding ACs, posture, principles, and the two hazards that are genuinely design-level because they can destroy data or mislead.

**R3 addendum.** Round 3 returned six more, and two changed the deliverable rather than the prose. F1 showed the seven onboarding caller sites are mutually exclusive branches, so no DB scenario can cover the class — which is the argument for the static meta-test, and the reason it is the oracle here rather than a nicety. F6 refuted a safety claim this spec had made outright: `_metaDestructiveDbTargetGuard` discovers only `reset_validation_data`, and `_metaLocalDbUrlGuard` scans only `LOCAL_TEST_DATABASE_URL`, so neither covers a prune test connected through `TEST_DATABASE_URL`. The spec had cited both as already-handling the hazard. Extending the destructive-target guard's discovery is now part of the deliverable.

That is the round-economy lesson of this arc in one line: the findings that cost the most rounds were the ones where the spec asserted a protection instead of shipping it.

**R4 addendum, and the structural response.** Round 4 returned five findings and **not one was about the design** — all five were assertion or guard completeness. That is the measurement this filing predicted after R3, so it was acted on rather than argued with: the spec's §5 assertion inventory moved to the plan, leaving posture, the failure each requirement prevents, and the hazards that are design-level because they can destroy data or return an empty result indistinguishable from the bug.

Two of the five changed the deliverable rather than the prose, and both are the same shape as R3 F6 — a control asserted rather than shipped:

- **F4** found a real hole in the structural defense added after R2. Its recognizer was hand-listed and omitted the two canonical exported writers, while `app/api/cron/sync/route.ts:4,21` imports and wires `writeSyncLog` directly. A plain call evaded both the recognizer and the registry with no obfuscation. The recognizer is now derived from the module's exports — the same "derive a cover, don't enumerate" rule the class-sweep discipline already required, applied to the guard that exists to enforce it.
- **F3** showed the guard extension promised after R3 was necessary but insufficient: `CALLS_LOCAL_GUARD` matches a call *whose name looks right*, without establishing that the guarded value is the URL handed to `postgres`, that the call precedes the connection, or that the callee is the real import.

**The pattern across all four rounds, stated once:** every finding that cost a round was a place where the document asserted a protection instead of shipping one — a rationale for explicit-wins that had the causality backwards (R1), a "complete" list that was not (R2, R3, R4), meta-guards cited as covering a hazard they never matched (R3), and a recognizer enumerating shapes instead of deriving them (R4). The prose was confident in exactly the places the mechanism was absent. What converged the arc was not more prose but three shipped mechanisms: a probe, a derived recognizer, and a CI-keyed anti-vacuity condition.

**R5 addendum — the restructure worked, and the last finding was the biggest.** Round 5 returned two findings against the design-only spec, down from five, and both were design rather than assertion-shaped. That is the measurement the R4 move was making a bet on.

F1 is the largest real defect the arc found, and it arrived at round five: `SyncLogDeps.logSync` is optional and optional-chained, and **no production manual entry point supplies a sink** — so manual applied/skipped/error outcomes write no `sync_log` row at all. Not a NULL show id; no row. The spec's headline claim, that `observe synclog --show` answers correctly, was false for the operator's most deliberate action, and would have shipped that way. Two comments in `useRaw.ts` assert the logging happens, which is part of why it went unnoticed for five rounds — including by this author, who cited manual sync as the path that *did* record the show back when the arc began.

Why review caught it and the author did not: every earlier check asked "does this writer attribute?" and none asked "is a writer installed at all?" The existing test injects `logSync` itself, so it proved a test-only path. A guard over emission sites cannot see a missing injection — a lesson that changes the meta-test's scope, not just this spec.

**The arc's pattern, restated with the last data point:** every finding that cost a round was a place where something asserted a protection instead of shipping one. Rounds 1-3 found that in the spec's prose. Round 4 found it in the guard the spec added. Round 5 found it in a source comment. The defect class was constant; only its host changed.

**R6 addendum — the same class, a fifth time, and what finally answers it.** Round 6 returned two findings. F1 found two more sinkless entry points (`unarchiveShow.ts:24` via the unarchive action, and `pull-sheet-override/route.ts:213` whose `runSync` defaults to the real function), making eight rather than six. F2 found that the factory rule written one round earlier matched no actual code: the only production invocation is `makePostgresSyncLogSink(sql)(entry)`, immediately invoked with no binding, while the rule said to track the binding it is assigned to.

Both are the arc's signature defect: **a hand-listed cover that is short.** The tally is now five — the writer recognizer (R4 F4), the guard's own exemption (caught in authoring), the verification grep used to check a fix, the six-site census, and now the census again after it had already been corrected once.

The lesson is not "enumerate more carefully." It is that enumeration was the wrong instrument every single time, and that each round spent extending a list bought exactly one round of safety. What ends the class is deriving the cover: the writer set from a module's exports, the entry-point set from the exported entry points, the emission site from the AST shape rather than a name. Those are now the contract, and the lists that remain in the documents are labelled illustrative so no future reader mistakes one for the guarantee.

**R7 addendum — review found a bug in a different feature, and the arc stopped rather than absorbing it.** Round 7 returned three findings. F1 was mine to fix and embarrassing in a specific way: the spec fenced "an aliased import" out of scope as obfuscation while simultaneously relying on catching alias-based calls — a rule contradicted by its own threat model. All four indirect sinkless paths use exactly that shape (`catchUp`, `runSync`, two DI properties), so the fence excluded the only cases that mattered. Corrected: the rule keys on the import, and an alias is ordinary authoring, in scope by construction.

F2/F3 are not this change's to fix. The existing-show pending-ingestion retry path throws `SyncInfraError` before any sync work, because `runManualSyncForShow_unlocked` calls `processOneFile_unlocked` with five arguments and that function requires six. A sink cannot fix a path that throws first. Filed as `BL-PENDING-RETRY-EXISTING-SHOW-THROWS` and fenced out of AC-6 explicitly, rather than left quietly false.

That is the disposition rule working as intended, and worth recording because seven rounds of accepting findings can build momentum toward accepting all of them: the class-sweep default is repair-in-PR, but exception (a) — needs a design decision this PR cannot settle — is a real exception, and a broken retry flow is a different defect class from a missing sink. The arc's job was to make attribution true, not to fix everything review walked past.

**R8 — the scope question the arc can no longer answer for itself.** Round 8 returned four findings. Three were mine to fix and are fixed: the import rule was file-existential where it had to be per-call (F2); the entry-point seed set had no accept-set, so seeding on every export would have made a guard import a false positive (F3); and a numeric claim said seven NULL-duration writers where the table has nine (F4, a plain numeric-sweep miss by the author of a numeric-sweep rule).

F1 is different in kind. It is probe-backed and it says the sink repair is insufficient: `runManualStageForFirstSeen` returns before its sole emission on the stage / hard_fail / pass / defer branches, and `runManualSyncForShow` awaits `runOne` with no catch, so a throw escapes unlogged. The reviewer's probe reported `logSyncCalls: 0` for four branches and for the throwing case. Manual sync is therefore not merely unattributed — it is largely unemitted, on branches a sink cannot reach.

That converts the remaining work from "attribute the show" into "make manual sync observable at all," which is a materially different change from the one this arc was authorised to make. Eight rounds in, the honest reading is that review has been discovering that the surrounding feature is more broken than the spec assumed, one layer at a time: no show id, then no sink, then no emission. Each layer was a true finding. None of them was the change that was approved.

The arc stops here and asks. Not because a rule requires it — the disposition rules would permit absorbing this as a class sweep — but because the scope decision is the user's, and eight rounds of correctly accepting findings is exactly the momentum that makes a run decide one alone.

**R9 — first non-blocking verdict, and one last instance of the arc's own defect.** NEEDS-ATTENTION with three MEDIUMs, all repaired.

F1 was the third revision of one derivation: the accept-set said an entry point is an export reaching `processOneFile`/`processOneFile_unlocked`, which excludes `runManualStageForFirstSeen` because it calls `emitSuccessfulPhase2Tail` directly. Two hand-picked function names were still a hand-list wearing the word "structural". It now derives against the writer set — an entry point is anything that can cause a write — which collapses two derivations into one and is the property the rule actually cared about.

F2 is the one worth recording plainly. In round 8 I stated, in the round message and in the plan, that the recognizer had been narrowed to inline object literals to kill three measured false positives. **It was never written into the spec.** The reviewer's `rg "inline object|object literal"` over the artifact returned nothing. That is precisely the failure this filing has been documenting for nine rounds — a mechanism asserted rather than shipped — committed by the author who had by then named it five times. Worth stating because the lesson is evidently not "know the rule": I did, and wrote it down, and still shipped the claim without the change.

F3 is a real measurement limit now documented rather than implied: `duration_ms` is the runner boundary, and for the first-seen retry path the expensive preparation happens before the timed runner, so a slow export with a fast apply records only the tail. An injected-delta assertion inside the runner cannot detect it, which is why it is documented rather than tested away.

**R10 — one finding, and it was the derivation working correctly.** BLOCKING/1. Widening the accept-set to writer-reachability (the R9 F1 repair) admitted a surface I had not accounted for: `applyStaged` invokes `emitSuccessfulPhase2Tail` and its two live callers inject no tail dependencies, so a first-seen staged apply records no attempt row.

That is not a new defect class. It is the third instance of the one the user fenced in §6.2, found because the derivation is now general enough to reach it — which is the argument for derivations over lists, arriving as evidence rather than as an assertion. The fence and `BL-MANUAL-SYNC-UNEMITTED` were extended to name it, with the reviewer's `rg` showing no production injection of `firstPublishedTailDeps` anywhere.

The disposition detail worth keeping: these sites get `// sync-log-emission-gap: BL-MANUAL-SYNC-UNEMITTED`, deliberately NOT the run-level marker. A run-level marker asserts no show is knowable; a staged apply knows its show perfectly well. Reusing it would have laundered a filed defect into a design claim, which is the arc's signature failure wearing a different hat.

**R11 — two MEDIUMs, both corrections to the previous two rounds' repairs.** NEEDS-ATTENTION/2.

F1 is an over-correction of mine. Having found a third `applyStaged` caller in round 10 I assigned it the gap marker along with the other two — a correct enumeration paired with the assumption that every caller shared the defect. It does not: the wizard route fixes `sourceScope: "wizard"`, stages approval and manifest metadata only, and never reaches the tail; the show apply happens at finalize. The code says so in its own comment. Enumerating correctly and then classifying carelessly is a new way to be wrong in an arc that has mostly been wrong by enumerating short.

F2 is a plain self-contradiction: §3.6 said each site occupies "exactly one of two states" while §6.2 had introduced a third. Implementing the list literally would reject every legitimate gap marker. Fixing it exposed a second propagation gap — §3.6 still carried the *file-level registry* wording that had been replaced with site-precise markers in the plan and the pre-written meta-test rounds earlier. Two artifacts had the fix; the spec did not.

That is the same shape as R9 F2 and as this tick's own marker propagation, now three times: **introducing a mechanism in one artifact and mistaking that for shipping it.** The arc's most durable lesson is not about covers or enumerations at all — it is that a change is not made until every artifact that must honour it says so, and the only reliable check is to grep for it rather than to remember making it.
